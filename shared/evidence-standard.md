# Shared Evidence Standard

## Source hierarchy

Prefer, in order appropriate to the question:

1. Current guidance from recognised clinical, public-health, or professional bodies.
2. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses with suitable methods and populations.
3. Consensus statements and position stands that disclose methods and conflicts.
4. Randomised controlled trials.
5. Strong prospective observational research.
6. Mechanistic, acute, animal, or laboratory evidence only when its limitations are explicit.

Use original papers and official guidance for technical claims. Search for the newest relevant evidence when information may have changed.

## Appraisal checklist

Check population match, sample size, duration, comparator, adherence, attrition, preregistration, outcome switching, absolute effect, confidence interval, adverse events, funding, conflicts, replication, and whether the outcome matters to the user or is only a surrogate.

Distinguish statistical significance from practical importance, association from causation, and absence of evidence from evidence of no effect. Do not elevate a single striking study above the totality of stronger evidence.

## Men’s Health and other media

Use Men’s Health for approachable framing, exercise ideas, recipes, or discovering claims to investigate. Trace each material health claim to the underlying study or authoritative guidance. Check whether the headline exaggerates causality, size, certainty, or applicability. If the underlying source cannot be verified, call the claim unverified and do not base high-stakes guidance on it.

## Reporting

Lead with the verdict or practical recommendation. Label confidence as high, moderate, low, or insufficient, and explain the main limitation. Cite sources directly and close to the claims they support. Never invent a paper, DOI, author, quotation, result, or consensus.


## Recency and citation convention

For a project whose value is being **accurate and up to date**, treat freshness
as part of the evidence, not an afterthought.

- **Date every consequential claim.** State when the source was checked, e.g.
  "(checked 2026-09)". Guidance changes; an undated claim is a stale claim
  waiting to happen.
- **Name the guideline version.** Cite the specific body and version/year, e.g.
  "NICE CG181 (2023 update)" rather than "NICE guidance".
- **Prefer the newest applicable evidence** and note when a source is superseded.
- **Re-verify on use for high-stakes topics.** Screening ages, blood-pressure or
  lipid targets, drug interactions and supplement doses must be checked against
  current guidance at the time of advice — never asserted from memory.
- **Flag likely drift.** If a claim depends on figures that change periodically,
  say so and point the user to the authoritative source for the current value.
- **Applicability by region.** Confirm the guidance matches the user's country
  (see `user-profile.md`); note when US and UK guidance differ.

### Citation format (lightweight)

> Claim. — Source body, document/version, year (checked YYYY-MM). Link if available.

Never invent a paper, DOI, author, quotation, result, consensus **or date
checked**.
